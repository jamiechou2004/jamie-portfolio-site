02 / WHAT WE FOUND

Three gaps the manager
had to cover by hand.

01 / ORDERS

Manual and digital orders get mixed up.

Phone and walk-in orders were tracked apart from app orders, which led to kitchen errors.

02 / SYSTEMS

The POS doesn’t talk to inventory.

Legacy POS hardware had no link to modern inventory tracking.

03 / CHANNELS

Delivery runs outside the kitchen flow.

UberEats and DoorDash ran on separate tablets, apart from the kitchen’s own workflow.

FROM THE FIELD · GAP 03

Delivery issues live in a separate tool.

On a restaurant laptop, orders and customer-reported issues sat in the UberEats merchant site, away from the POS and the kitchen display.

04 / FROM OPPORTUNITY TO STRUCTURE

Merge delivery apps, phone and walk-in orders into one feed.

05 / FIRST DESIGN · TODAY’S SUMMARY

A useful overview
needs an order.

I organized the first dashboard around three questions: what needs attention, what explains it, and where to look next.

01 / ORIENT

Separate the channels.

In-store and online orders stay distinct in one view.

02 / UNDERSTAND

Make status visible.

Each order carries its state and timing, not just a total.

03 / CONTINUE

Keep details nearby.

Live notifications and order context support the next step.

Each answer traces back to a journey pain point: scattered feeds → separate channels; unclear queue → visible status; handoff mistakes → details nearby.

06 / TESTING

Clear to read.
Not enough to act.

I tested the Summary prototype with managers in five restaurants. They found it clear. Then they kept asking for the same thing.

5restaurants tested
80%positive feedback
WHAT WORKED
  • Managers found the summary clear and easy to read.
  • Separating in-store and online orders matched how they already split the work.
  • Some managers couldn’t reach customers directly, which caused delays.
WHAT MANAGERS ASKED FOR
  • “Let me define an action on each page.”
  • “Message customers directly from here.”
  • “Remind the supplier when inventory is low.”
  • “Help me know my customers’ habits.”

Three of four requests are about acting, not seeing.

The gap wasn’t information.
It was the step after it.

Seeing a delayed order still meant switching apps to fix it. My response was Define Action: a way to turn an insight into a next step without leaving the view.

08 / REFLECTION

Design around
the manager’s judgment.

The project shifted my focus from organizing a screen to supporting the work around it.

RESEARCH & PRODUCT DIRECTION

Study the work.
Find the need.
Shape the response.

Interviews helped me see a wider coordination task. The feature direction came from interpreting that work, not waiting for someone to request an Agent.

PRODUCT JUDGMENT

Give the Agent a purpose.

Let a specific task explain the move beyond a dashboard. A new capability needs a clear role in the manager’s work.

USER CONTROL

Design the way out, too.

Editing, declining, minimizing and recovering belong alongside the proposed action.

EVIDENCE

A prototype opens a question.

A complete flow shows the interaction intent. It does not establish better decisions or less setup effort.

NEXT VALIDATION

Test the decisions,
